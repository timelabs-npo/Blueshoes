THE MAP IS QUIET.
THE FLOW HAS TEETH.

A town has two bridges. Whoever controls the only remaining crossing can decide who gets home. Networks have bridges too: providers, gateways, names, permissions. Their power is easy to miss until everyone must pass through them.

Topology.What can reach what? Keep the roads and crossings; ignore the pretty drawing. Remove a bridge and see which places lose a path.
Geometry.How do those paths compare? Choose measurements: distance, delay, cost. The shortest-looking line can be the slowest way home. Capacity and policy add their own constraints.
Flow.What actually gets through? A path is a possibility. Traffic, messages and useful work turn that possibility into an event. Queues reveal whose time is being spent.

A small, invented example. Two independent bridges carry 4 and 3 vehicles per minute. Demand is 5; send 4 across one and 1 across the other. Close the 4-vehicle bridge and only 3 can pass. At least 2 must wait, be refused, or use another real crossing. Assume the access roads impose no smaller limit. This is arithmetic, not a router benchmark.

Flow Surgery is the ambition: change the supporting path while preserving the intended connection and its constraints. A model may suggest a crossing. It cannot draw capacity into existence.
